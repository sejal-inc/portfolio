A blog that fed the sales pipeline

The company sells expense management software to small and midsized businesses, mostly in the US. The blog was one of the main ways buyers found it, and the marketing team’s goal was qualified leads.

Writers owned the words. How the blog looked, read and was built was mine. I joined as an intern in July 2023, embedded in the marketing team, and went full time in May 2024.

The question I kept coming back to: how do you make long, dense finance content something people actually want to read?

Pink era · 2023 to 2024

The first system, and visuals that break up long posts

There was no blog design system when I joined, so I built the first one. While building it, I noticed the banners weren’t the problem. The posts were: long walls of text on dense topics.

I started designing infographics inside the posts, explaining a process in one picture instead of five paragraphs. They were live across the blog before the end of 2023. Two of the largest companies in the category then began breaking up their content the same way.

Purple era · 2024 to 2025

A recognisable look, easier navigation, and a report that underperformed

The company refreshed its brand in October 2024. I rebuilt the blog system with it, aiming for a look people would recognise before they saw the logo. Then I worked on how people move through a post. Each feature answered one reading problem:

LiveFeatureProblem it solves
Nov 2024Progress barReaders can’t tell how much is left
Jun 2025FAQsFollow-up questions go unanswered
Jul 2025Table of contentsReaders can’t jump to the part they need

I also designed our annual benchmark report almost end to end: the report, its landing page, supporting blogs and social posts. It mostly reached people already on our site. Well designed, and still underperformed, which taught me that who a report reaches, and in what form, is part of the design.

Green era · 2025 to now

From flat images to components, ready for AI search

After an acquisition, the company rebranded site-wide in October 2025. I rebuilt the system a third time, and turned the blog’s recurring visuals (calls to action, callouts, quotes, product reviews) from flat images into reusable CMS components, working with writers and developers through launch.

Images were slow to update, broke on phones, were invisible to search engines and AI, and couldn’t be measured. Components fixed all four.

~50%blog traffic lost as search moved to AI answers
−8%only, among readers ready to buy
AI > linksAI tools overtook backlinks as a referral source, with no extra spend

I can’t claim the components caused that. What I can show is that when search changed, the content was already in a form machines can read: text instead of images, clear sections and direct answers.

2025 to 2026

Measuring it: a failed first attempt, then a working system

My first attempt failed because visuals were images: there was no way to tie a number to one visual on one page. Components solved that. In autumn 2026 I built the system: a registry of 16 blog features, a map of every page each appears on, a clean baseline across four data sources, and a nine-page dashboard.

Desktop33%
All readers28%
Mobile19%

Readers who reach the halfway point of a post · September 2026 baseline

That gave the reading work a hard number to move, and pointed to mobile as the next problem.

The dashboard creates the shortlist. Testing creates the evidence.

What I’d carry into any team

  • Readability compounds. Work done to help people read ended up helping machines read too.
  • Build the system, not the shortcut. Images were faster to ship. Components scaled and could be measured.
  • Plan measurement before design. I couldn’t prove my early work because I hadn’t built a way to see it.
